# Why "Civilized Society" May Never Arrive

civility is not a choice. It is a condition. And that condition keeps getting further away.

An ancient Chinese text, *Guanzi*, written around the 7th century BC, offered a deceptively simple theory of civilization: *fill the granaries first, and people will learn manners. Clothe and feed them, and they will understand honor.*

The logic is hard to argue with. You cannot lecture a starving man about virtue. Dignity requires a baseline.

So here is the uncomfortable question: humanity is richer today than at any point in history. Why does "enough" keep receding into the distance?

---

## I. Why Ancient Granaries Could Actually Be Filled

In the ancient world, a granary was a physical thing. Grain, gold, land, paintings — all of it required space. This created a natural consequence: it had a ceiling. When it was full, it was full. To accumulate more, you had to take it from someone else, visibly, at personal risk.

Three mechanisms kept extreme concentration in check.

The first was **anonymity**. He Shen — the most corrupt official in Chinese imperial history, whose confiscated fortune was said to rival the entire treasury of the Qing dynasty — had no idea what everyone else had. Without a scoreboard, there was no scoreboard anxiety. If you felt rich, you more or less were.

The second was **forced reset**. Imperial power was the sword hanging over every fortune. When He Shen finally fell from grace, the Jiaqing Emperor feasted on the confiscation. Even when emperors were lenient, dynasties eventually collapsed and wealth was redistributed by violence. The system was brutal, but it had a clearing function. In the West, the French Revolution performed a similar service for the aristocracy, and Roosevelt's antitrust era put at least a temporary brake on the Gilded Age fortunes. Coercive, disruptive, often bloody — but they worked.

The third mechanism, and the one that deserves its own reckoning, was **the Church**.

Jesus said it plainly, in the Gospel of Matthew: *Do not store up for yourselves treasures on earth, where moths and vermin destroy. Store up treasures in heaven. For where your treasure is, there your heart will be also.*

This was the largest moral-restraint project in the history of Western civilization. Greed was one of the seven deadly sins. Usury was prohibited. A rich man entering heaven, Jesus warned, faces worse odds than a camel passing through the eye of a needle. For centuries, the Church deployed the full weight of divine authority to deliver one message to an entire continent: the unbounded accumulation of earthly wealth is sin.

By the height of the medieval period, the Church owned between a quarter and a third of all land in Western Europe.

The founder said it in writing. The institution did the opposite.

This is not an argument against religion. It points to something more fundamental: **any system that relies on moral self-restraint to limit accumulation will eventually be consumed by the very appetite it was designed to curb.** Every mechanism that ever actually worked was external, coercive, and usually violent.

## II. The Numbers: How Concentrated Has Wealth Actually Been

The following figures draw on the World Inequality Database (WID.world) and associated historical research:

| Period | Region | Wealth held by top 1% |
|--------|--------|------------------------|
| Early 1800s (~1820) | Western Europe | ~50–55% |
| Early 1900s (~1910) | Western Europe | ~55–60% (historical peak) |
| Post-war to 1970s | Western Europe & US | ~20–25% (historical low) |
| 2020s | United States | ~35–40% and rising |
| 2020s | Global top 0.01%* | Equivalent to the entire bottom 50% combined |

\*Figures for the final row use the 0.01% threshold because at global scale this group most starkly illustrates the degree of extreme concentration.

*Sources: Chancel & Piketty (2021), WID.world; Waldenström (2024); US Federal Reserve data. 19th-century estimates carry a margin of ±5–10 percentage points; the directional trends command broad scholarly consensus.*

A few things stand out. Inequality in 1820 was already staggering — the top 1% held more than half of everything. The 20th century then produced something genuinely rare: two world wars, the Great Depression, progressive taxation, labor unions, and the welfare state together forced the share down to its lowest recorded level. Since the 1980s, financialization, globalization, and digitization have driven it back up. The United States is now approaching pre-First World War levels.

The one period when granaries came closest to "full" — when a genuine middle class existed and wages actually tracked productivity — was roughly 1950 to 1980. It took the destruction of two world wars to create the political conditions for it. No one wants to repeat that experiment.

## III. Three Locks, All Broken

How was that brief equalisation undone? The answer lies in how the three ancient constraints were dismantled, one by one.

**The first lock: anonymity.**

He Shen didn't know what everyone else had, so rivaling the imperial treasury felt like an endpoint. Today, Bloomberg publishes real-time net worth rankings. Forbes tracks every billionaire on earth. Every major accumulator knows exactly where they rank and precisely how far behind the next tier they sit. This visibility transformed wealth accumulation from "enough" into an open-ended competition with no finish line.

**The second lock: forced reset.**

The emperor is gone. Law replaced him, but the direction reversed. Complex ownership structures, offshore assets, shell companies, and jurisdictional arbitrage have given extreme wealth a legal armor that no single government can easily pierce. When states attempt to pursue it, they face legal teams more sophisticated than their own tax authorities. No edict, no confiscation, no dynasty collapse.

**The third lock: physical space.**

Ancient wealth was material. There was always a day when you ran out of room. Today's wealth is digital — stocks, derivatives, cross-border accounts, intellectual property. It exists in a virtual space that scales without limit. A single individual can hold assets exceeding the GDP of a mid-sized nation without needing a single warehouse.

All three locks broken. The result is a degree of concentration without historical precedent. According to the World Inequality Lab's 2026 report, over the past thirty years the wealth of the global top 0.01% has grown at over 5% per year — nearly twice the rate of ordinary households. Today, approximately 56,000 people — a group that would fit inside a single football stadium — hold more wealth than 2.8 billion adults combined. Not a metaphor. A number.

---

## IV. So When Does "Civilized Society" Arrive

*Guanzi*'s logic rests on a hidden assumption: that granaries can be filled. Fill them, and people stop scrambling. Stop scrambling, and they find the space to become civilized.

But if the granary has no ceiling — if "full" has been engineered out of the system — then the filling never ends. Not because resources are scarce, but because the rules of the game have changed.

Jesus said: store your treasure in heaven. The Church said yes, and enclosed a third of Europe's land. The emperor said: you cannot exceed me. His successors said: there is no emperor. *Guanzi* said: fill the granaries and manners will follow. Capital said: there is no finish line.

Manners are still waiting.

*A note on scope: the wealth-share figures in the table refer primarily to Western Europe and the United States, where long-run historical data exist. Global figures for earlier periods are scholarly reconstructions with wider uncertainty ranges.*
