# On Living Clearly: A Map

Most people carry responsibilities — to family, to work, to the people around them. The conditions for monastic withdrawal are simply not there. More importantly, withdrawing from temptation is not the same as being free of it. There is a real difference between keeping one's temper when nothing provokes it and remaining steady when something does. True stability is not built by avoiding the test. It is built by passing through it.

Over the years I have watched many sincere seekers spend their energy on forms, rituals, and organizations, only to find themselves drifting further from whatever brought them there in the first place. This essay is written out of that concern. After decades of exploration, reflection, and practice, I have arrived at a simple approach — simple enough to be walkable — and I share it here with those who may find it useful.

## What Cultivation Means, and What Three Traditions Contribute

Cultivation, as I use the word here, means the sincere effort to raise one's state of awareness and move toward clarity. Throughout history, teachers and sages have left guidance: Laozi described the destination but left no method; Zhuangzi pointed at the destination and the path through story and parable; the Buddha provided both a clear destination and a clear method.

Of all these teachings, the Buddha's account of ignorance and ego-clinging is the most precise description of how the mind actually operates. Knowledge helps us understand the world; emotion helps us engage with it — both are natural and useful. Incomplete or distorted knowledge leads to misreading, wrong conclusions, and misguided action: this is ignorance. Joy, anger, sorrow, delight — all are genuine human feelings. But clinging to them, unable to let go, is ego-clinging. Together, these two generate unnecessary friction between people, and between people and the world, distorting both awareness and behaviour.

One note worth making: the Buddha did not design his path only for monastics. Among his four assemblies of disciples, laypeople were always an essential part. Vimalakirti, the central figure of the Vimalakirti Sutra, is the classic example of a layperson who reached deep realization. What this essay draws on is the Buddha's diagnosis of how the mind works — not the monastic way of life.

## The Destination: Following the Natural Order

The Diamond Sutra is clear: those who seek the Buddha through form or sound are on the wrong path. The heart of the matter has never been in outward form. It is in inner awareness.

For the destination, I align with Laozi: following the natural order. The natural order, as I mean it here, is not mysticism. It is the way things actually work — the laws of the physical world, the patterns of living and growing, the principles that govern how people relate to one another. This destination is not cold. It does not ask for the elimination of feeling, or the abandonment of family. It asks only that feeling return to its natural state — without clinging, but not suppressed. Following this way requires neither giving up family and social responsibilities nor flattening the full range of human experience. Living one's life well is enough.

When Laozi says "act without contrivance, yet leave nothing undone," he does not mean passivity. He means: stop acting against the grain of things, and nothing will be beyond reach. After his awakening, the Buddha did not stop feeling. He retained compassion — he had left suffering himself, and wished to help others do the same. At a deeper level, this meets the spirit of *ren* at the centre of the Confucian tradition.

## The Starting Point: Confucius and Mencius, with *Ren* at the Core

For the starting point, the teachings of Confucius and Mencius are the most practically walkable. Among the five cardinal virtues — benevolence, righteousness, propriety, wisdom, and integrity — propriety and wisdom may be interpreted differently across cultures and eras. But *ren* — genuine care and goodwill toward others — is the core. It stands in no essential conflict with the Buddhist ideal of *cíbēi* or the Daoist ideal of goodness.

Starting with *ren* means first fulfilling basic obligations: to family, to friends, to the people around you — acting with sincerity, doing one's best. For those with no capacity beyond this, it is already a complete life. Those with further capacity may go further: examining and loosening ignorance and ego-clinging, developing greater clarity, observing how the natural order actually runs, and acting in accord with it.

## The Particular Value of Staying in the World

The greatest value of this path is that it is real. Every day brings real temptation, real conflict, real gain and loss — and with them, real ignorance and real ego-clinging. Every moment of awareness within that reality, every small transcendence, is genuine progress. This is fundamentally different from cultivation in a protected or sequestered environment: withdrawal reduces friction, but friction is the only thing that produces actual growth.

At the same time, those who walk this path while remaining in the world can still fulfil their responsibilities to family and society, and still enjoy what life offers. This is not an obstacle to the path. It is part of what the path is for.

## No Need to Cling to the Destination

Each person's circumstances, capacity, and inclinations are different. This essay offers a map, not a required destination. Most people may never reach the end of it, and that is entirely fine. Allowing each person to stop wherever they find meaning and growth is itself an expression of following the natural order.

This is a distillation of my own decades of exploration and practice. I share it because I find it works. Nothing more is intended. This path requires no affiliation with any religion or organization, no rituals, no teachers, not a single cent. All it requires is a person who sincerely wishes to walk it. We are all somewhere along the road. There is no need to cling to the destination.
