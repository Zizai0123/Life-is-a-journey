# Veiled by Avidya and Ego-Clinging: An Eternal Struggle in the Parent–Child Relationship

What is discussed here is a phenomenon that occurs universally in normal family life — a long-standing difficulty that nearly every family with children will encounter. It has nothing to do with whether parents are good or bad, or whether children are virtuous or flawed. It is caused by the very mechanisms of human psychology: the workings of avidya and atman-graha.

A girl has a fight with her mother and storms out of the house in anger. Exhausted and hungry, she goes to a small restaurant and asks for a glass of water. The owner makes her a bowl of noodle soup. She drinks it, and is moved to tears. The owner asks why. She says she was touched by his kindness. He asks why she doesn't go home. She says her mother doesn't understand her. He says: I made you one bowl of soup, and you were moved to tears. But what about the person who, three hundred and sixty-five days a year, every single day, thinks carefully about what to cook for you, tries to make you happy, and puts every one of your genuine needs before her own — have you ever once been moved by her?

All human feelings, perceptions, and emotions are products of evolution. They exist for a reason: to help us become aware of our own condition. Reality is impermanent — birth, ageing, illness, and death; reunion and separation; joy and sorrow — these are all natural phenomena that no one can escape.

Avidya is the obscuring of how things work and how cause and effect operate. Atman-graha is placing one's own feelings and goals at the centre, while ignoring external conditions and the circumstances of others. The two are often entangled: avidya obscures causation, atman-graha reinforces attachment, and together they form the deepest barrier in the parent-child relationship.

One root of avidya is that human beings cannot perceive absolute values — we can only feel change, typically the gap between expectation and reality. With those close to us, expectations are high, while reality often merely meets or falls short of them; the gap is zero or negative, and so we feel dissatisfied. With strangers, expectations are low; most of the time reality is similarly low, and there is no dissatisfaction. But on the rare occasion when reality exceeds expectation, the gap turns positive — and we are moved to tears.

A parent's attentive care, given twenty-four hours a day and three hundred and sixty-five days a year, gradually fades into the background precisely because expectation always equals reality — the gap is always zero, and the value perceived approaches nothing. Sometimes expectation even exceeds reality; the gap turns negative, and the parent's efforts earn not gratitude but complaints of nagging and misunderstanding. "An indulgent mother raises a wayward child" — the old saying does not deceive us.

Another root of avidya is the severing of the chain of cause and effect. The universe, the solar system, human society, the family — all operate according to rules, the result of billions and thousands of years of stable functioning. Fail to follow those rules and consequences follow. For a child to truly understand that boiling water burns, the only real way is to be burned once — language and text are not the same as truth, because they carry no felt experience. What traditional education calls responsibility training and adversity education is, at its core, education in complete chains of cause and effect, focused on the relationship between long-term investment and outcome. To sever the causal chain and consider only the immediate feeling — divorced from what gave rise to it and from what it will eventually produce — is very dangerous, and typically leads to immature decisions.

Atman-graha also manifests as overgeneralisation: letting one feeling override all others. A single unpleasant argument can obscure years of warmth and ordinary memory on either side, and ultimately destroys both the capacity to perceive reality clearly and the capacity to experience happiness. This typically happens when a child cannot forgive a parent. It is rare for a parent not to forgive a child, because a child, from infancy onward, has always been on a path of continuous growth and progress.

There is another common pattern in adolescents: complaining that parents and teachers "don't understand me." Yet what lies behind this demand for "understanding" is usually a search for "validation" — a validation severed from cause and effect, severed from the basic moral and ethical values that human beings share. If this kind of validation is constantly reinforced, it will eventually obscure basic judgment about how things work and how cause and effect operate.

Atman-graha in adolescence often takes a different form: whatever parents approve of, the teenager instinctively resists; whatever parents oppose, the teenager insists on. This looks on the surface like building identity and pursuing independence, but in reality parents remain the only frame of reference — the direction is simply reversed. A genuine self is not built through rebellion against others, but through knowledge of oneself. To define oneself through rebellion is still a form of atman-graha — just pointing in the opposite direction.

There is also a common form of avidya called the externalisation of responsibility: when something goes wrong, the first response is always that it is the parents' fault, the environment's fault, someone else's fault — never an examination of one's own role in the causal chain. This is closely related to the severing of the causal chain, but not identical: severing the chain means not seeing consequences; externalising responsibility means seeing the consequences, but attributing the cause elsewhere. The two together produce a deeply entrenched form of avidya — neither learning from the past nor taking responsibility for the future.

An example: a child skips school to play video games. Parents will inevitably reproach; teachers will inevitably criticise; gaming companions will inevitably encourage and praise. If the child is veiled by avidya and atman-graha, they will conclude that their gaming companions are the "real friends who truly understand me," while parents and teachers become enemies. As for the act of skipping school to play games, the feeling of "being happy right now" is real and undeniable. But for the observance of school rules, the accumulation of knowledge, the preparation for the future, and the direction of one's life, that "being happy right now" is very likely quietly creating harm; and if we consider the anxiety, disappointment, and worry of the parents, this feeling is inevitably harmful to the whole family.

Yet avidya and atman-graha do not belong only to children. Most ordinary parents treat their children as the most precious thing in the world, give everything they have, and do their best to create the finest life they can for them. But from this deep love, tendencies toward overprotection and over-giving are almost inevitable; without self-awareness, they too will find it difficult to escape the trap of avidya and atman-graha.

The most common avidya in parents is indulgence — especially when the parents themselves grew up in hardship, and tend to give doubly in order to compensate for the deprivation they once experienced. Yet love without rules, without responsibility, without cause and effect, will ultimately damage a child's patterns of behaviour, making them wilful and self-centred, lacking the ability to follow rules and cooperate with others. The deeper the love, the deeper the veil it casts when awareness is absent.

There is another form of parental avidya: not understanding the psychological states and behaviour patterns of children at different ages, and holding young children to the standards of older adolescents or even adults in a way that is wholly disconnected from reality — conflict and frustration will inevitably follow.

There is also a form of parental atman-graha: "I" have given "you" everything — feeling, time, money, energy — and therefore "you" should do as "I" say. This too severs the causal chain, pressing the feeling of "I have given everything" down over the child's genuine needs. It is simply atman-graha redirected.

There is a form of parental atman-graha even more concealed than "sacrifice in exchange for control": treating the child as an extension of the self, using the child's life to fill in one's own unfinished regrets. Forcing the child to study piano, because one never managed to learn it oneself. Insisting the child pursue a career in government, because one never managed to do so oneself. This form of atman-graha is often wrapped in the deep tenderness of "it's for your own good," and the parents themselves may not even notice it. Yet a child is an independent life — not a sequel to the parents' story. Possession in the name of love is still atman-graha.

There is also an extremely common form of parental avidya: the comparing mind. Unable to see the child's real qualities and genuine growth, the parent can only sense gaps through lateral comparison — "other people's children" become a measuring rod suspended permanently overhead. This is identical to what was described at the beginning of this essay about the inability to perceive absolute values, only shifted from child to parent. The comparing mind severs the parent's perception of the child's true condition, and it severs the child's capacity to feel their own worth — a child who is never good enough in their parents' eyes will find it very difficult to truly believe that they are.

All of the above describes a predicament that humanity has experienced and re-experienced across thousands of years. But today, parents and children face an unprecedented external condition as well: carefully engineered algorithmic systems and certain social currents — ones that invoke the language of protection while severing cause-and-effect and responsibility — are continuously feeding and reinforcing avidya and atman-graha from the outside, making veils that were already present go deeper, become harder to notice, and harder to penetrate. To remain clear and aware in such an environment is an unprecedented challenge that every parent and child in this era must seriously face. The avidya and atman-graha being carefully cultivated around us are more deeply rooted, more concealed, and more difficult to perceive than anything that came before — and to penetrate them requires more arduous and more sustained effort from parents and children alike.

Avidya and atman-graha are common human veils, present almost everywhere. But if one can observe and understand the patterns by which things operate — such as the psychological states and behavioural patterns of children at different ages, and the ways parents and children can relate to one another — and at the same time remain aware of how avidya and atman-graha work, then in daily life, in the very moment when conflict arises and emotion surges, pausing for just a few seconds and observing carefully — whether parent or child — there is still the possibility of seeing clearly whether one is currently veiled, and making a choice that fully understands the causes and consequences, and for which one is willing to bear responsibility. That girl who ran away from home — if one day she is able to pause for just a moment when anger rises, she may come to see: what moved her to tears was a single bowl of soup, while three hundred and sixty-five days of care left her feeling nothing — because avidya and atman-graha were quietly at work. To see that is the beginning.
