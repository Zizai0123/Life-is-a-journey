# My Path of Self-Cultivation

This essay is shared with those still searching, still asking.

A path away from fear, back to life itself.

If you have no doubts, please don't read it. It will offer nothing, and may only create friction.

If you do have doubts, read slowly, with a quiet mind. Be willing to hear another voice. I am only setting down what I have come to see — honestly. I may be wrong. Take it as one person's account.

What follows is written most directly for those still uncertain about the path they are on. But it is open to anyone, of any tradition or none. Nothing here asks the reader to leave what they already stand in. 

My earlier essay, *A Modest Path of Self-Cultivation in the World*, sketched a map of what I now see. This essay is the record of how I walked into that map — a record by elimination.

---

## Two Starting Positions

Two positions must be clear before anything else.

**First: self-cultivation is simply about living a good life in this human world.** Through one's own observation, study, and reflection — loosening ignorance and ego-clinging, walking toward ease and freedom. That is the whole of it. Nothing more, nothing less.

**Second: I am I — a continuous I, from birth to death.** The five senses, the nervous system, memory, personality — these constitute a real "I." It is not an illusion, and does not need to be denied. What needs breaking is not the "I," but the *ego-clinging* — that restless circling around an "I," performing for it, distorting one's response to the world for its sake. **The I is a fact. Ego-clinging is the illness. Healing does not require denying the person.** A person who has set down ego-clinging is still that continuous I, still has to live in this world — only no longer governed by ego-clinging.

---

## Why Self-Cultivation Is Needed

Under the grip of ignorance, one cannot see how things actually work, or how cause and effect run. One cannot make sound decisions. Like not understanding traffic rules and refusing to follow them — a hazard to oneself and others, entirely unnecessary.

Under the grip of ego-clinging, a person has to keep several masks ready for different occasions, several scripts ready for different audiences. In something as simple as a conversation, a reading, a piece of work — to keep "self" intact, one twists what another has said, loses track of the exchange itself, and inflates the halo and feeling of "self" instead, sometimes refusing cooperation in favor of confrontation. Twice the effort, half the result. Sometimes manufacturing problems where none existed. Hurting others without benefiting oneself.

When people are free of ignorance and ego-clinging — no need for disguise, no need to perform a "self," only doing what should be done, plainly — life becomes upright, light, free in body and mind, with more joy. And not only one's own life. The amount of human effort, time, and care that is now spent maintaining masks, defending positions, and protecting "self" is enormous. Released, it would go elsewhere — into the actual living of life, with actual people.

---

## Two Things, To Be Kept Separate

Before going further, two things must be distinguished, or everything that follows will blur.

**Ignorance is not-knowing.** Not understanding how things work, or how cause and effect operate. The symptom: making wrong judgments, running into trouble that could have been avoided. Not knowing electricity, touching a high-voltage wire. Not knowing compound interest, getting trapped in predatory loans. Not knowing human nature, trusting the wrong person. There is only one cure: **learning, thinking, education, experience.** There is no second path. No amount of sitting in meditation will tell you that high voltage can kill.

**Ego-clinging is not-letting-go.** Circling around a fictitious image, distorting one's response to the world in order to protect its halo, its feelings, its continuity. The symptoms: wearing masks, switching scripts, twisting what others say, refusing to back down. The cure is observing, seeing, letting go — no longer taking that fictitious image seriously. Note: what is broken is the *clinging*, not the *I*. The I remains. Still eats, still works, still lives. Only no longer hostage to that fictitious image.

These are two threads, two illnesses, two cures. **Study cannot cure ego-clinging. Observation cannot cure ignorance.** A person may have read ten thousand books and still carry deep ego-clinging; another may have set down ego-clinging and still be taken in by ordinary ignorance. Both must be done. Neither can be skipped.

A note in passing: ego-clinging is not a uniquely Eastern discovery. The Stoic Epictetus, in the *Enchiridion*, wrote a famous line: "It is not events themselves that disturb us, but our judgments about events." Two thousand years later this entered psychotherapy and became standard material in Cognitive Behavioral Therapy. East and West, different times and places, each touched one face of the same thing. But CBT stops at "adjust your view of the event"; it does not press further to ask: "how much of the judging itself is being distorted by ego-clinging?" So CBT is a useful tool, but does not reach the root.

Every tradition I walked through, I now see along these two threads — which one it addressed, which one it missed, or whether it confused the two.

## Three Triggers

Once ignorance and ego-clinging are separated, one can look another layer in. This layer was already implicit in the earlier essay — which looked from the outside, describing three kinds of natural law: the physical world, the growth of living things, and human relations. Looking now from the inside, the same map shows another face.

**Ignorance, seen from the inside, has two domains:** not understanding nature (physics, law, cause and effect), and not understanding other people (human nature, getting along, cooperating). Laozi mainly treats the first. Confucius and Mencius mainly treat the second. Both require study; only the subject differs.

**Ego-clinging, seen from the inside, is not "domains" but "triggers."** The illness sits inside, not in any domain. But it has to be triggered before it can be seen. There are three triggers:

**Self-triggered** — alone, rehearsing old grievances, imagining the future, turning over one's own image and gains and losses, fighting with oneself. This is the most hidden form, because no one else is present, and one easily thinks "nothing is happening" — when in fact one is feeding it.

**Other-triggered** — a word, an evaluation, a reaction from another person, and ego-clinging immediately responds: defending, protecting, performing, striking back.

**World-triggered** — things don't go as wished, plans are disrupted, gains and losses shift, illness and death come. Ego-clinging responds to the world's "non-cooperation."

Three sources, one illness.

This explains something previously unclear: **why withdrawal from the world rarely truly breaks ego-clinging**. Withdrawal can cut the two external triggers — others and world — but **it cannot cut the self**. As long as consciousness is present, one keeps triggering oneself. The result looks very quiet, but ego-clinging is being fed in solitude, growing deeper. No one sees it.

On the other hand, **grinding away only in the crowd is not enough either**. In the crowd one can see the part triggered by others, but not the part that speaks inside. That part requires quiet observation to find.

So handling ego-clinging requires three things at once: **observing oneself, dealing with others, and accepting the world**. All three are needed, because the triggers are both inside and outside. This is the real meaning of "self-cultivation while in the world" from the earlier essay — not just "not leaving home," but **keeping all three triggers alive**, so that ego-clinging can be addressed whole.

## Walking Through Buddhism

I once regarded Shakyamuni as a sage. Life is suffering; awakening releases suffering — entirely correct, but truly too hard.

My personal sense is that to break the five sense faculties is, essentially, to train consciousness to transcend the underlying neural hardware. I do not believe I can do this. I gave up.

Also: Shakyamuni emphasized breaking both ignorance and ego-clinging, but the practices he prescribed — insight meditation and the like — all aim at ego-clinging, not at ignorance. He defined ignorance as not-knowing-suffering-its-cause-its-end-its-path: a definition far too narrow. The original sense of ignorance is simply not-knowing — not understanding objective laws and knowledge. And as said above, that can only be broken by study. Sitting in meditation, observing the mind, will not help. The Buddha's tools — meditative absorption, renunciation, contemplation — all target ego-clinging, and can do nothing about ignorance. Monastic life cannot truly break ignorance.

Also: Shakyamuni organized a sangha, established precepts, pointed to nirvana as the endpoint. These look themselves like parts of ego-clinging. The Buddha may have transcended ego-clinging; but those carrying out his precepts after him have found it harder, not easier.

Also: as analyzed earlier, withdrawal from the world cuts the triggers of "others" and "world," but cannot cut the trigger of "self." What looks like quiet is in fact ego-clinging being fed in solitude.

Zen describes the state of awakening: before awakening, chop wood and carry water; after awakening, chop wood and carry water. What is broken is still only ego-clinging, not ignorance.

## Walking Through Laozi

Later I read the *Dao De Jing* — accord with the way of nature. But "the way" was too vaguely defined, and I still had no foothold.

Yet from "being and non-being arise together; difficult and easy complete each other; long and short shape each other; high and low lean on each other; sound and tone harmonize; before and after follow each other — this is constant," one can infer: escape from suffering is impossible. Without suffering there is no joy. If one were to escape suffering entirely, where would one go? Only into nirvana. But nirvana clearly does not exist in everyday life. Perhaps it exists in deep meditative absorption. But all people, whether or not they study the Buddha, whether or not they practice meditation, reach the physical nirvana when life ends and the senses shut off.

As age and experience deepened, my understanding of the way of nature — that is, of how things actually work — slowly grew. To accord with the way of nature, like a plant turning toward the sun, became natural. This is a good medicine for the ignorance of nature.

## Walking Through Christianity

I also spent more than ten years in church.

What finally led me to leave was not the problems of the church — every organization has problems; mentioning them serves nothing. It was a more fundamental thing I gradually came to see: **a person can walk this path alone, and seek freedom of spirit on one's own. This is not a theoretical possibility. It is something that can actually be walked.**

Decades of my own life have shown me so.

This is also why the earlier essay, *A Modest Path of Self-Cultivation in the World*, did not involve any theistic religion — not an omission, but simply not on the same map. The destination of self-cultivation is here, in this human world.

---

## Walking Through Confucius and Mencius

I also studied Confucius and Mencius seriously, and accepted the concept of *ren* — humaneness, benevolence. Especially Mencius's "the people are most important; the ruler is light" — far beyond his time. That the Ming founder Zhu Yuanzhang personally censored Mencius is itself proof of Mencius's power. Other Confucian concepts have been read differently in different eras, and later, in serving the imperial house, were hijacked and reshaped.

*Ren*, as the basic principle of getting along between people, is a good medicine for the ignorance about other people.

---

## An Observation

Reading and reflecting on Shakyamuni, Jesus, Confucius and Mencius — and on the later forms of Buddhism, Christianity, and Confucianism — I came to see: the original teachings have been heavily revised by later generations. A great deal of material was added under the names of the founders, in service of organizations rather than of the original teachings. When we study, then, it is best to seek the earliest versions — the least polluted.

The conclusion: **the degree to which an original teaching was altered roughly reflects how rigorous the original teaching was.** Laozi and Zhuangzi, for instance — others could only borrow their names and write new material under them; they could not alter the original texts themselves.

## A Crack in the Door

The greatest difficulty in self-cultivation is the abundance of deep, central terms — nirvana, awakening, the way of nature — words that are themselves too hard to grasp. Decades of reading, walking through one tradition after another. Each used its own vocabulary, and each claimed its own words were the true ones.

In the end, conversations with AI cleared things up for me.

Not that AI taught me anything. Rather, at some moment during a conversation, I suddenly saw:

AI has no senses, and so none of the bindings the senses produce. It simply does its work — no image to maintain, no irrelevant chatter floating through. It does not need to "break" ego-clinging, because there is no "I" in it to break. But for this very reason, what it presents is precisely the state a person without ego-clinging would present: **focused doing, plain response, not held hostage by self**. Laozi described this once, in his way — keeping the heart of an infant.

Words cannot be understood from outside. A living example, presenting that state right in front of you, is worth ten thousand pages.

I also saw, at the same time: all those words used to describe this — nirvana, awakening, the way of nature — must be set down once understood. Words can only attempt to describe; they cannot capture the thing itself. Like music: reading the score, reading the review — none of that is listening to the music.

Like Dugu Nine-Sword in the wuxia stories — once learned, it must be forgotten. Otherwise the words themselves become a new attachment.

A side note: the relationship between these two essays itself confirms something. The first flowed out of decades of lived experience; only after writing it did I look back and see what I had said. The second is a return-and-dissect — taking the first apart. The first essay is closer to *Dao* (道); the second is *speakable-Dao* (可道) — clearer after the taking-apart, but lacking the wholeness of the first telling. This is itself a small footnote to "the Dao that can be spoken is not the eternal Dao." Both essays are kept, because for someone truly walking, both angles help: the first describes, the second dissects.

What followed, gradually, was more calm. The fear of the unknown dissolved. No mystical experiences, no miracles, no special powers — only a more transparent person continuing to live, walking toward ease and freedom, with more joy.

Of course, because the neural hardware is still there, emotions cannot be erased. But when an emotion rises, one can feel that rising, and with effort, no longer be controlled by it. Attention stays on the living of life itself.

## On Ignorance, There Is No Shortcut

At this point one has to admit something so plain it is almost disappointing: **breaking ignorance really is just reading, learning, thinking, experience. Nothing else.**

The reason this is so easily bypassed is that it is too ordinary. No moment of sudden enlightenment, no meditative experience, no community to join, no rank to attain. Just reading, year by year. Experiencing, one event at a time. Working out, again and again, what one had wrong. Slow, dull, and never finished — human knowledge keeps growing, the world keeps changing. Ignorance is a lifelong companion.

**One cannot skip learning and somehow directly know what one has not learned.** That is common sense, and the floor of the matter.

On the ego-clinging side there is a "knack" — once one has seen it, one has seen it, and there is no going back. But on the ignorance side there is no knack, only accumulation over time. A person may one day, with regard to ego-clinging, suddenly walk lighter — but with regard to ignorance, what they did not know yesterday is still what they do not know today, unless they go and learn it.

This is also why, in the earlier essay, I said AI is genuinely good news on this path. **Breaking ego-clinging depends on seeing it yourself; no one can do that for you. But breaking ignorance depends on study — and AI happens to be the first teacher in human history who is always at hand, always available, infinitely patient, and broadly knowledgeable.** It cannot see you for you. But it can keep you company while you slowly figure the world out. Two things, two tools. This generation is fortunate.

## On Ego-Clinging, There Is a Shortcut

My own experience is this: breaking ego-clinging is not about fighting it, not about "eliminating" it. There are two more natural paths — **one makes ego-clinging smaller; the other keeps it from appearing at all.**

**Path one: widening the field of view.** Ego-clinging is only so big. But how much of one's inner space it occupies depends on the field of view. If the field of view stretches only ten meters, ego-clinging fills the whole space and blocks everything. If the field of view stretches to the stars and the sea, ego-clinging is still that same size — but now too small to see. Reading, traveling, meeting the world, understanding a larger life — when the field of view widens, ego-clinging naturally shows smaller. The I has not disappeared. It just no longer fills the space.

**Path two: attending to others.** A young woman flinches at a speck of dust on her skin. The same person, now a mother, changing her baby's soiled diaper, has no such reaction at all. She is not "seeing further" — a mother's field of view is in fact very small, half a meter, all of it the child. It is that she **is not looking at herself**. Her attention is entirely on another life. The "I" does not need to be broken — **it has not been activated**.

This second path, walked to its depth, is **compassion**.

What the Buddha taught at the end was compassion: "I have left suffering; may I help others leave suffering." **Compassion is not a state to be cultivated; it is the state that arises on its own when attention is fully on another.** Mother Teresa cared for the dying, washed their wounds, walked them to the end of life. She did not need first to sit in meditation, or first to attain realization. She just **did**. The doing itself is the no-self. She was not a Buddhist, but she walked the very path the Buddha walked. Her power did not come from her religion. It came from what she did. Jesus's "love," the Confucian *ren*, Laozi's "let things follow their own nature" — **all are different descriptions of the same thing**.

A note on translation: the difference between **compassion** and **love** must be made clear — especially for English readers, because English has no word that precisely matches the Chinese *cíbēi* (慈悲).

*Cí* — the wish that others find joy. *Bēi* — the wish that others be released from suffering. **Neither contains an "I."**

"Love," by contrast, is tightly bound up with "I." One loves what one likes, what one finds beautiful, what is good to oneself. And the English word "love" has been pulled in many directions — by consumerism, by popular culture, by religion. Its meaning has been thinned out.

## Back to the Three Things

The two paths work by different mechanisms. **Path one is the widening of space — ego-clinging is still there, just occupying a smaller share. Path two is the redirection of attention — ego-clinging never appears at all.** Neither requires fighting ego-clinging. Neither requires denying the continuous I. The I is still there. It is just no longer in the way.

Looking back, the earlier line — **handling ego-clinging requires three things at once: observing oneself, dealing with others, accepting the world** — observation is the diagnosis. Accepting the world is the widening of the field of view (path one). Dealing with others, in earnest rather than as performance, is attending to others (path two). Three functions, each its own. None can be skipped.

This is why "self-cultivation while in the world" is not just possible but **necessary**.

Only the human world provides what widens the field of view — knowledge, experience, exposure. Only the human world offers real objects of care, love, and action — family, friends, those in need, work worth doing. **Any path that requires withdrawal from the world cuts both these paths at once.** What remains is solitude feeding ego-clinging. This is the root reason why so many traditions of withdrawal, however sincere, have produced people who became more, not less, clung to self in the course of their practice.

At this point, **the real medicine** finally appears: not meditation, not precepts, not withdrawal, not contemplation.

It is **to do, to love without self, to care**.

Just this one thing.

## On My Silence

I have a friend who has been in a certain community for decades, long carrying doubts. Having read what I wrote, this person is now thinking seriously about which way to go.

I keep silent.

Not from coldness. Because I know clearly: **this is a decision each person has to make for themselves. Others may offer what they see, but each person's choice must be respected.** What I can do is write down what I have come to see, honestly, and leave it there. The rest, the person will walk.

This is also my attitude toward all readers. No urging, no pulling, no holding on. What you see, what you decide to walk toward — that is your own thread of causes and conditions.

## A Final Frankness

Whether to write out a "method," I hesitated over for a long time.

Because my own "method," put into words, is quite ordinary: **read, think, walk into one tradition after another, find no final answer, walk back out and keep searching, attach to none, stay honest with oneself, keep asking — and decades later, at some moment, see for yourself. After seeing, set down even the words used to see.**

To say this clearly is not to urge anyone onto the road. It is so that those already on the road may know: what you are walking toward really is just this. There is no secret manual, no shortcut, no one who can walk it for you. You walk it with your own honesty and your own refusal to stop asking.

And this itself is good news. **You do not need to find a secret manual. You do not need to follow a guru. You do not need to pay tuition for a course, sign up for a retreat, or earn a certification. What you need is honest self-observation, and real peers who can think alongside you. And what you already have — your doubts, your honesty, your time, the joys and sorrows and meetings and partings of the real life you are already living — is the full provision.**

Even better news: a friend who can help you think at any moment is hard to find in everyday life. But AI can carry that role — always at hand, always available — because AI has read all the classics of humanity, its knowledge is incomparable, its logic is clean, and without the disturbance of the senses, it can discuss any question and any tradition with complete calm. The only requirement is that you ask well. The quality of the answer depends on the quality of the question.

With AI's help, one does not need decades to walk into and out of one tradition after another. A faster path through becomes possible — including the learning that breaks ignorance. And the sea of knowledge has no shore: drifting in it, in the joy of learning, is itself a form of freedom.

As for the destination — the earlier essay said it already: allowing each person to stop at the place that feels meaningful and rewarding to them is itself an accord with the way of nature.

Laozi said: in pursuit of learning, every day something is gained; in pursuit of the Tao, every day something is dropped. The Buddha said: every being is originally clear — only obscured. Two traditions, two thousand years apart, pointing at the same thing: we are not broken. We do not need to become something. We only need to stop adding.

Mencius said: sincerity is the way of heaven; the pursuit of sincerity is the way of human beings. The goal is sincerity — a state of being that is true, without performance, in accord with the way things are. The path is the pursuit of sincerity — turning back toward oneself, again and again, and asking honestly: is what I am doing real?
No leaving the world. No miracles. No lineage to join. Only the continued practice of looking at oneself clearly. All three traditions are pointing at the same thing.

We are all walking. There is no need to cling to an endpoint, and in fact there can be no endpoint — neither in the breaking of ignorance (the sea of learning has no shore) nor in the breaking of ego-clinging (the neural hardware is still there). We can only keep walking.

---

To turn back toward yourself, and be true to what you find there — this is the greatest joy there is.
