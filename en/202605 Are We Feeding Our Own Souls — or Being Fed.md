# Are We Feeding Our Own Souls — or Being Fed?

## On how we consume information, and what we're quietly losing

There's an old Cherokee parable: a grandfather tells his grandson that two wolves live inside him, locked in constant battle. One is good. One is evil. The boy asks: *Which one wins?* The grandfather answers: **The one you feed.**

We all carry those two wolves. The question is what we're putting in the bowl.

---

## What the Kid Said

There's a story about a child raised in a wealthy household who was asked where rice comes from. He answered: **from the pot.**

The modern version is almost a cliché: ask a city kid where milk comes from, and he'll say **the grocery store.**

Neither child is wrong, exactly. Neither is lying. They're simply reporting what they've seen. The rice did appear in the pot. The milk did come from the store. They just never encountered the source — the field, the cow, the labor between. **They only ever saw the finished product.**

Here's the uncomfortable part: we are becoming that child. Not just about food. About almost everything.

We don't know where our emotions come from. We don't know how relationships actually grow. We've never watched someone die. We've never sat with real poverty. We've never traced a thought from its muddy origin to a clear conclusion.

We only see the **finished, packaged, focus-grouped version** — and we mistake it for the real thing.

---

## The Extraction Chain

The history of how humans receive information is, if you squint at it, a history of one process: **distillation.**

**Books** were the original format — and the least processed. No images supplied for you. No soundtrack cueing your emotions. No verdict handed down. You brought your own imagination, built the world from words, sat alone with the silence between sentences. Books trained patience. Precision. The ability to tolerate ambiguity. The willingness to be changed slowly.

Their great gift was **the gap** — the white space between what's written and what you make of it.

**Television serials** gave us something different: the long arc. Decades of a character's life, episode by episode. You learned to follow a thread across years. But the director made every visual decision for you. *Slowly, the muscle for building your own pictures began to atrophy.*

**Film** compressed further. Two hours. A tight emotional journey. The cause-and-effect was still there, but backstory was cut, subtext collapsed, and commercial movies trained us to expect a clean resolution. We began to believe **every story should have a tidy ending.**

**Short-form video** is the terminus. Thirty seconds to a payoff. Three seconds to a hook. What gets left on the cutting room floor: complex relationships, consequences, time, context, the experience of boredom.

But the deepest loss isn't attention span. It's **narrative self-possession** — the ability to understand your own life as a story with a beginning, a long uncertain middle, and an ending you haven't reached yet. You can't tell that story if your brain has been rewired to skip to the highlight reel.

At every step, the medium did a little more of your thinking for you — and made sure you enjoyed it before handing any of that work back.

---

## What's Being Extracted

This isn't only about screens. The same process is running across every dimension of experience:

Love → romance films → pornography → a three-second clip.

Food → cooking → restaurants → delivery apps → meal replacement shakes.

Friendship → growing up together → occasional visits → text threads → a like on a photo.

Travel → exploration → guided tours → watching someone else's five-second reel.

Family → living under one roof → FaceTime → holiday gift cards.

Death → happening at home → happening in hospitals → happening in the news → not happening at all.

Nature → farming → a walk in the park → a desktop screensaver.

Same process, every time. The extracted product is more intense, more convenient, more reliably satisfying. And **increasingly disconnected from the original thing.**

Eventually you're living in a world of pure concentrates — everything maximally stimulating, frictionless, exactly what you expected. And the real versions — real love, real food, real friendship, real life — start to feel **bland, difficult, and disappointing.** Because your baseline has been calibrated to the extract. You've lost the taste for the source.

---

## A Concrete Picture

Imagine someone who grew up marinating in short-form content.

He's absorbed thousands of hours of what love is supposed to look like — the cinematic declaration, the perfect chemistry, the frictionless desire. He's been trained by hours of content that edits out everything in between.

Then he meets an actual person.

She gets tired. She has bad mornings. She worries about the electric bill. She goes quiet sometimes for no reason he can decode. She is, in other words, **a full human being.**

He doesn't know what to do with that.

It's not that he's incapable of love. It's that **he was never trained to love something unedited.** His nervous system learned to respond to the highlight reel. Real life doesn't have one. So he swipes away.

And it's not just romance. It's work — real work has long, unremarkable stretches with no visible progress. It's parenthood — a real child doesn't deliver a tender moment every three seconds. It's aging parents — real aging is slow and unglamorous and sometimes embarrassing. It's his own inner life — which can only be heard in silence, and which has nothing flashy to offer.

**He fails at everything that requires raw ingredients.** Not because he's lazy. Not because he's cruel. Because *he's never handled raw ingredients.*

He's the child who said the milk comes from the store. His blind spot just covers almost everything.

---

## Nobody Planned This

No one called a meeting and said: *let's make people less capable.*

But the incentives don't need a villain. Platforms optimize for time-on-screen, not for your development. Algorithms reward stimulation; they penalize quiet. The human brain is a shortcuts machine, and dopamine doesn't ask about quality. Markets serve the lowest common denominator, and the denominator **keeps moving down.**

Mike Judge's film *Idiocracy* was marketed as a comedy. Watched now, it feels more like a nature documentary. The people in that movie didn't choose stupidity. They just, generation by generation, chose the easier option — *until no one remembered there had ever been another one.*

---

## What Can Actually Be Done

Against a systemic force, does individual choice matter?

Yes. Not because one person can reverse the tide — but because **this is happening inside your own neurons.** Every time you pick up a book instead of your phone, you are reinforcing one neural pathway and letting another go thin. Every time you sit across from a real person and talk without a script, without an algorithm curating the exchange, without instant feedback — you are rebuilding a capacity that is quietly disappearing.

Language is what separated us from the rest of the animal kingdom. Not strength. Not speed. **The word.** Writing let knowledge accumulate across generations; no one had to start from nothing. Books were the primary vessel for that inheritance for thousands of years — every insight, every grief, every hard-won lesson compressed into those pages.

Going back to books isn't nostalgia. **The act of reading is itself the rebuilding.**

Building a world with no images given to you.
Forming a judgment with no answer provided.
Staying in a dull passage without leaving.
Holding patience across a long arc.

These are not quaint habits. They are **a fundamentally different mode of being** — and they produce a fundamentally different life than passive consumption does.

Books and real human contact are the two most powerful re-entry points we have.

Read something that takes time. Sit down with someone real — no script, no algorithm, no instant feedback loop.

These two things are vanishing from children's lives at the same rate. The phone displaced the book and the neighborhood at once. A child who has never learned to resolve a real conflict, who has never had to invent something to do on a slow afternoon — that child hasn't just lost an entertainment option. **Two of the oldest channels of human development have closed simultaneously.**

---

## The Two Wolves

Back to where we started.

Feed the wolf that reads, that engages with real people, that tolerates the uncomfortable middle of things — and it grows. It gets better at navigating darkness. Better at understanding others, at understanding history, at understanding why your own life is the shape it is.

Feed the wolf that needs the next hit, the next hook, the next three-second payoff — and it grows hungry in a different way. Permanently hungry. Incapable of sitting still for a slow conversation, a long project, a love that asks something of you. When life presents those things — and it will — **the response is panic, then distraction, then moving on.**

Neither wolf dies. But *your life will increasingly resemble the one you've been feeding* — intentionally or not.

---

The child said the milk comes from the store because no one had ever shown him the field.

We are becoming that child. **The difference is we don't know it's happening.**

*The choice is still in your hands. But the window, as with most things worth keeping, may not stay open indefinitely.*
