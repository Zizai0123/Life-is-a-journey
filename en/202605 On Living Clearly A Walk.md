# On Living Clearly: A Walk

*For those still searching, still asking.*

A path away from fear, back to life itself.

If you have no doubts, please stop here. Nothing in this essay will help you, and it may only create friction.

If you do have doubts, read slowly, with a quiet mind. This is one person's account — honestly set down, possibly wrong. Take it as such.

My earlier essay, *On Living Clearly: A Map*, sketched what I now see. This essay is the record of how I walked into that map — a record by elimination.

---

## Two Positions

Two things must be clear before anything else.

**First: this is about how to live well in this human world.** Through one's own observation, study, and reflection — loosening ignorance and ego-clinging, walking toward ease and freedom. That is the whole of it. Nothing more, nothing less.

**Second: I am I — a continuous I, from birth to death.** The five senses, the nervous system, memory, personality — these constitute a real self. Not an illusion. Not something to be denied. What needs to be broken is not the self, but *ego-clinging* — that restless circling around a self-image, performing for it, distorting one's responses to the world on its behalf. **The self is a fact. Ego-clinging is the illness. Treating the illness does not require denying the person.** Someone who has set down ego-clinging is still that continuous I, still living in the world — only no longer governed by it.

---

## Why Bother

Under the grip of ignorance, one cannot see how things actually work. One cannot read cause and effect. One makes avoidable mistakes — like not understanding electricity and touching a live wire.

Under the grip of ego-clinging, a person keeps several masks ready for different occasions, several scripts ready for different audiences. In something as simple as a conversation or a piece of work together, to protect the self-image, one twists what the other person said, loses track of the exchange itself, and sometimes chooses confrontation over cooperation. Twice the effort, half the result. Sometimes manufacturing trouble where none existed.

Free of both: no need for disguise, no need to perform. Just doing what should be done, plainly. Life becomes lighter, more honest, more joyful.

---

## Two Things, Kept Separate

Before going further, two things must be distinguished. Blur them and everything that follows will be confused.

**Ignorance is not-knowing.** Not understanding how things work, or how cause and effect run. The symptom: wrong judgments, avoidable trouble. Not knowing electricity, touching a high-voltage wire. Not knowing compound interest, getting trapped in predatory loans. Not knowing human nature, trusting the wrong person. There is only one cure: **learning, thinking, education, experience.** No second path. No amount of sitting in meditation will tell you that high voltage kills.

**Ego-clinging is not-letting-go.** Circling around a fictitious self-image, distorting one's responses to protect its halo, its feelings, its continuity. The symptom: masks, shifting scripts, twisting what others say, refusing to back down. The cure is seeing — observing, recognizing, no longer taking the fictitious image seriously. Note: what breaks is the *clinging*, not the self. The self remains. Still eats, still works. Only no longer hostage to a fiction.

Two threads, two illnesses, two cures. **Study cannot cure ego-clinging. Observation cannot cure ignorance.** A person can have read ten thousand books and still carry deep ego-clinging. Another can have set ego-clinging down and still be taken in by ordinary ignorance. Both must be done. Neither can be skipped.

A note in passing: ego-clinging is not a uniquely Eastern discovery. The Stoic Epictetus wrote in the *Enchiridion*: people are disturbed not by events but by their judgments about events. Two thousand years later, this entered psychotherapy as Cognitive Behavioral Therapy. East and West, different times and places, each touched one face of the same thing. But CBT stops at "adjust your view of the event." It does not press further to ask how much the judging itself is distorted by ego-clinging. Useful tool. Doesn't reach the root.

Every tradition I walked through, I now read along these two threads — which one it addressed, which one it missed, or whether it confused the two.

## Three Triggers

Once ignorance and ego-clinging are separated, one can look one layer deeper.

**Ignorance has two domains:** not understanding nature — physics, law, cause and effect — and not understanding people — human nature, getting along, cooperating. Laozi mainly treats the first. Confucius and Mencius mainly treat the second. Both require study. Only the subject differs.

**Ego-clinging is not about domains. It's about triggers.** The illness lives inside. But it needs to be triggered before it can be seen. Three triggers:

**Self-triggered** — alone, rehearsing old grievances, imagining the future, turning over one's image and gains and losses, arguing with oneself. The most hidden form. No one else is present, so one easily thinks nothing is happening. In fact, one is feeding it.

**Other-triggered** — a word, an evaluation, a reaction from someone else. Ego-clinging responds immediately: defending, performing, striking back.

**World-triggered** — things don't go as wished, plans are disrupted, illness and death arrive. Ego-clinging responds to the world's refusal to cooperate.

Three sources. One illness.

This explains something I couldn't see before: **why withdrawal from the world rarely truly breaks ego-clinging.** Withdrawal can cut the two external triggers — others and world. But **it cannot cut the self.** As long as consciousness is running, one keeps triggering oneself. The result looks very quiet. But ego-clinging is being fed in solitude, growing deeper, with no one watching.

On the other hand, **staying only in the crowd isn't enough either.** In the crowd one can see the part triggered by others, but not the part that speaks inside. That requires quiet to find.

So working with ego-clinging needs three things at once: **observing oneself, dealing with others, accepting the world.** All three triggers must stay open. This is what "cultivation in the world" actually means — not just staying in the world, but keeping all three channels on.

## Walking Through Confucius and Mencius

I studied Confucius and Mencius seriously. The concept of *ren* — benevolence, genuine care for others — I find true. Mencius in particular: "The people are the most important; the ruler is the least." That was radical. The fact that the founding emperor of the Ming dynasty personally edited Mencius out of the canon is its own testimony to how powerful that idea was.

The later Confucian tradition was hijacked — reshaped to serve imperial authority, drifting far from its origins. That is a different thing.

*Ren* as the foundation of human relations is the medicine for ignorance about people.

---

## Walking Through Buddhism

The Buddha's diagnosis of ignorance and ego-clinging is the most precise account I have found of how the mind operates. The Kalama Sutta offers the clearest principle for independent inquiry.

I once regarded the Buddha as a saint. *Life is suffering; awakening is release from suffering* — both ring true. But the path he prescribed is genuinely hard.

My own reading: transcending the five senses is essentially training consciousness to override the neural hardware. I concluded I am not capable of that, and let it go.

The Buddha emphasized breaking both ignorance and ego-clinging, but the practices he taught — meditation, contemplation, withdrawal — all address ego-clinging. They do not address ignorance. The Buddha defined ignorance narrowly as not knowing the Four Noble Truths. But ignorance in its basic sense is not-knowing — not understanding how the world works. And that, as I said earlier, can only be fixed by learning. Sitting in meditation cannot teach you what you have not studied.

There is also this: the Buddha organized a monastic community, established precepts, pointed to nirvana as the destination. All of these, in a certain light, look like ego-clinging in institutional form. The Buddha himself may have transcended it. But those who come after, enforcing the precepts, often find themselves more entangled, not less.

And withdrawal cuts two of the three triggers — others and world — but not the self. The result is familiar: very quiet on the surface, ego-clinging fed in solitude, growing deeper unseen.

Zen describes the enlightened state as: before awakening, chop wood, carry water; after awakening, chop wood, carry water. What breaks is ego-clinging. Ignorance is not mentioned.

## Walking Through Laozi

I came to the *Tao Te Ching* later. Following the natural order — but the natural order was defined so loosely that I could not find a foothold.

Still, from "being and non-being arise together, difficult and easy complete each other, long and short define each other, high and low depend on each other" — one can see: freedom from suffering is impossible. Without suffering there is no joy. Remove suffering entirely and there is nowhere to go except nirvana. But nirvana is not available in ordinary daily life. And in a physical sense, everyone arrives there eventually, regardless of practice, when life ends and the senses shut down.

What did open up, over years, was a deepening sense of the natural order — how things actually work, how patterns run. Following that order became as natural as a plant following the light. The medicine for ignorance about the natural world.

---

## Walking Through Christianity

I spent more than a decade in a church.

I left not because of the church's institutional problems — those exist in every organization, and cataloguing them is not useful. I left because I gradually saw something more fundamental: **a person can walk toward inner freedom on their own. It is possible.**

Decades of experience tell me this is not just theoretically possible. It is practically walkable.

This is also why the earlier essay included no theistic religion — not an oversight. It is simply not on the same map. The destination is still here, in this human world.

---

## One Observation

Reading the original teachings of the Buddha, Jesus, Confucius, and Mencius — and then looking at what Buddhism, Christianity, and Confucianism became — the pattern is consistent. Later generations modified the original teachings heavily, adding their own material, operating under the founders' names while actually serving institutional interests. So when studying any of these traditions, go to the earliest available sources. Least contaminated.

A rough principle: **the degree to which an original teaching has been altered tends to reflect how rigorous that teaching was.** Laozi and Zhuangzi are an example — others could borrow the name and the banner, but could not alter the texts themselves. They had to write new content instead.

## The Crack

The greatest difficulty in all of this is the vocabulary. Nirvana, awakening, the Tao — these words are themselves nearly impossible to understand. Decades of reading, one tradition after another, each with its own language, each claiming its terms are the real ones.

Then, after walking through Confucius and Mencius, the Buddha, Laozi and Zhuangzi, Christianity — after reading the Kalama Sutta — through many long conversations with AI about practice and belief, something shifted. Not gradually. A crack opened.

Not because AI taught me something. It was something I saw in the middle of a conversation:

AI has no senses. So it has none of the entanglements the senses produce. It simply does the work — no image to protect, no unrelated thoughts drifting through. It doesn't need to "break" ego-clinging because it never had the self that clings. And precisely because of this, what it presents is exactly the state a person would present after setting ego-clinging down: **focused on the work, responding plainly, not hijacked by itself.** Laozi called it keeping the mind of a newborn child.

Words I had read for decades, I suddenly saw in a living example in front of me. One instance, in the present, was worth more than ten thousand words.

I also saw this: all the language used to describe the destination — nirvana, awakening, the Tao — once understood, must be set down. All words are attempts to point. None of them are the thing. Like music: reading a score, reading a review — neither is listening.

Learn the sword style, then forget it. Otherwise the words become a new attachment.

One more thing about these two essays. The first one came out in under an hour — decades of experience flowing out on their own. I didn't understand what I had said until I read it back. The second is the analysis — taking the first apart. The first is closer to *dao*. The second is *ke dao* — speakable, therefore no longer quite the thing. *The Tao that can be spoken is not the eternal Tao.* Both essays are worth keeping, because for someone genuinely walking, both angles have use: the first describes, the second dissects.

Afterward, more quiet arrived. The fear of the unknown dissolved — not through a mystical experience, no visions, no special powers. Just a cleaner person continuing to live, moving toward ease and freedom, with more room for joy.

The nervous system hardware is still there. Emotions still arise. But when they do, there is now a moment of seeing the change — and in that moment, a choice not to be carried.

## On Ignorance: No Shortcut

Here is something plain enough to disappoint: **breaking ignorance really is just reading, learning, thinking, living. Nothing else.**

It gets bypassed easily because it is too ordinary. No moment of sudden insight, no meditative experience, no lineage to join, no attainment levels to climb. Just reading year after year, living one thing after another, thinking through each mistake until it becomes clear. Slow, unglamorous, and never finished — human knowledge keeps growing, the world keeps changing. Ignorance is a lifelong project.

**No one can skip learning and know what they haven't learned.** That is common sense. It is also the floor under everything here.

Ego-clinging has a hinge — at some moment one sees, and once seen it cannot be unseen. But ignorance has no hinge. Only accumulation. A person may one day travel lighter from ego-clinging, but what they didn't know yesterday, they still don't know today, unless they go and learn it.

This is why AI is genuinely good news on this path. **Breaking ego-clinging requires seeing for oneself — no one can do it for you. But breaking ignorance requires learning — and AI is the first teacher in human history that is always available, infinitely patient, and knows an extraordinary range of things.** It cannot see you for you. But it can help you understand the world, piece by piece. Two different tasks, two different tools. This generation is fortunate.

## On Ego-Clinging: There Are Shortcuts

My own experience: breaking ego-clinging is not about fighting it or eliminating it. There are two more natural paths — **one makes it smaller, one makes it irrelevant.**

**Path one: widen the field of view.** Ego-clinging is only so big. But how much of your inner space it occupies depends on how wide you can see. If the field of view stretches only ten meters, ego-clinging fills everything. If it stretches to the stars, ego-clinging is still the same size — but now too small to see. Reading, traveling, meeting the world, understanding a larger life: when the field of view widens, ego-clinging naturally shrinks. The self has not disappeared. It just no longer fills the room.

**Path two: attend to others.** A young woman flinches at a speck of dust on her skin. The same person, now a mother, changes a soiled diaper without reaction. She has not "seen further" — a mother's field of view is actually very small, half a meter, all of it the child. The difference is that **she is not looking at herself.** Her attention is entirely on another life. The self doesn't need to be broken — **it was never activated.**

This second path, followed to its depth, is *cíbēi* — the Chinese word usually rendered as "compassion," though the translation is imprecise.

*Cí*: the wish that others find joy. *Bēi*: the wish that others be released from suffering. **Neither contains an "I."**

"Love," by contrast, is bound up with the self. One loves what one likes, what one finds beautiful, what is good to oneself. And "love" in English has been pulled in many directions — by consumerism, popular culture, religion. Its meaning has been diluted.

What the Buddha taught at the end was *cíbēi*: "I have left suffering; may I help others leave suffering." **It is not a state to be cultivated. It is what arises on its own when attention is fully on another.** Mother Teresa cared for the dying, washed their wounds, walked beside them to the end. She did not first sit in meditation, or first attain realization. She simply **went and did it.** The doing itself is no-self. She was not a Buddhist — but she walked the same path the Buddha walked. Jesus's "love," the Confucian *ren*, Laozi's "let things follow their own nature" — **different words for the same thing.**

---

## Back to the Three

The two paths work differently. **Path one widens the space — ego-clinging is still there, just smaller. Path two redirects attention — ego-clinging never appears.** Neither requires fighting it. Neither requires denying the continuous self. The self is still there. It is just no longer in the way.

Looking back at the three things — observing oneself, dealing with others, accepting the world — observation is the diagnosis. Accepting the world is widening the field of view. Dealing with others, in earnest rather than as performance, is attending to others. Three functions. None dispensable.

This is why living in the world is not just possible for this path. It is **necessary.**

Only the world provides what widens the field of view — knowledge, experience, exposure. Only the world offers real objects of care, love, and action — family, friends, people in need, work worth doing. **Any path that requires withdrawal cuts both routes at once.** What remains is solitude feeding ego-clinging. This is why so many traditions of withdrawal have produced people who became, over the course of practice, more entangled rather than less.

The real medicine, then, is not meditation, not precepts, not withdrawal, not contemplation.

It is **to do, to love without self, to care.**

Just this.

## On Silence

I have a friend who has been inside a certain community for decades, carrying doubts all along. Having read what I wrote, this person is now thinking seriously about which direction to go.

I keep silent.

Not from coldness. Because I know clearly: **this is a decision each person has to make for themselves.** Others may offer what they see, but the choice must be respected. What I can do is write down honestly what I have come to see, and leave it there. The rest, the person will walk.

This is also my attitude toward every reader. No urging, no pulling, no holding on. What you see, what you decide to walk toward — that is yours.

## A Final Frankness

Whether to write out a "method," I hesitated over for a long time.

Because my own method, put plainly, is quite ordinary: **read, think, walk into one tradition after another, find no final answer, walk back out and keep going, attach to none, stay honest with yourself, keep asking — and decades later, at some moment, see for yourself. After seeing, set down even the words you used to see.**

I am not writing this to urge anyone onto the road. I am writing so that those already on the road may know: this is what it looks like. No secret manual, no shortcut, no one who can walk it for you. You walk it with your own honesty and your own refusal to stop.

And this is good news. **You do not need a secret manual. You do not need to follow a teacher. You do not need to pay for a course. What you need is honest self-observation, and genuine peers who can think alongside you. And what you already have — your doubts, your honesty, your time, the joys and sorrows and meetings and partings of the life you are already living — is the full provision.**

Even better news: a friend who can help you think at any hour is hard to find in everyday life. AI can carry that role — always at hand, always patient — because it has absorbed an extraordinary breadth of human knowledge, reasons clearly, and without the interference of the senses, can discuss any question calmly and fully. The only condition is that you ask well. The quality of the answer depends on the quality of the question.

With AI, one does not need decades to walk into and out of each tradition. A faster path through becomes possible — including the learning that clears ignorance. And the sea of knowledge has no shore: drifting in it, in the joy of learning, is itself a kind of freedom.

As for the destination — the earlier essay said it already. Allow each person to stop where they find meaning. That itself is following the natural order.

Laozi: in pursuit of learning, every day something is gained; in pursuit of the Tao, every day something is dropped. The Buddha: every being is originally clear — only covered over. Two traditions, two thousand years apart, pointing at the same thing. We are not broken. We do not need to become something. We only need to stop adding.

Mencius: sincerity is the way of heaven; the pursuit of sincerity is the way of human beings. The goal is sincerity — a state of being true, without performance. The path is turning back toward oneself, again and again, and asking honestly: is what I am doing real? No leaving the world. No miracles. No lineage to join.

We are all walking. There is no need to cling to an endpoint — and in fact there cannot be one, whether in breaking ignorance (learning has no shore) or in breaking ego-clinging (the neural hardware remains). We can only keep walking.

*To turn back toward yourself, and be true to what you find there — this is the greatest joy there is.*
